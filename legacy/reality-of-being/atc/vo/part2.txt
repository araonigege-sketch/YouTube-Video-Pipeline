And at some places it’s far worse than one in three. Picture ten people walking into that academy together. By the time it’s done, more than three of them are already gone. Now send the survivors to a center like Oakland, out in California, and nearly half of the ones still standing never finish certifying. Start with ten, and you might end up with four.

And these aren’t people who wandered in off the street. They already passed the aptitude screen, the medical, the security clearance, and months of training. They failed anyway, because the thing being tested can’t be studied for. Trainees describe living in a low, constant fear that one mistake in a simulator ends the entire career they uprooted their life for. One man in training developed high blood pressure. His sleep fell apart. And that’s before he ever touched a live plane.

So why does anyone chase this? Follow the money, because the money is the tell.

An academy trainee starts around $45,000. Certify at a real facility and the number climbs fast, past six figures, and at the busiest towers and centers it keeps going. The overtime alone is staggering. At the most understaffed facilities, controllers pull in tens of thousands of dollars in overtime in a single year, on top of base pay. In 2026, this one job got a federal pay raise carved out specifically for controllers, at a time when most of the government wasn’t getting anything special. They’re dangling twenty percent bonuses at their most experienced people, the ones already old enough to retire, just to get them to stay one more year. And then another.
