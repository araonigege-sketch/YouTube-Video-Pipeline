Hey there! Picture this. You’re at the airport, waiting at your gate, and two people walk past in dark uniforms. White shirts, black ties, gold stripes on the sleeves, a small rolling bag behind each of them. They walk like they know exactly where they’re going. And half the people at the gate turn their heads as they pass.

Those are airline pilots. In about forty minutes, they’ll lift a machine that weighs about as much as fifty cars into the sky, with around 150 people on board.

So how do you become one of them? Do you need to be a genius? Do you need perfect eyesight? How much does training cost, and how long does it take? And here’s the part that surprises most people. Even after you earn a license that lets you get paid to fly, you could still be years away from an airline job. Why?

In this video, we’ll cover what pilots do, what they earn, how hard flight school is, what it costs, and how you finally get hired. Let’s get started.

So, what does an airline pilot actually do? On almost every airliner, there are two of them up front. The one on the left is the captain, who’s in charge of the aircraft and makes the final call on every decision. The one on the right is the first officer, basically the co-pilot. But the first officer isn’t up there to hold the coffee. Both pilots are fully trained to fly that exact plane, and on most trips they take turns. One flies, the other monitors, and on the next flight they swap.

Their work starts before takeoff. They check the weather, flight plan, fuel, and maintenance notes. One walks around the plane, checking the tires, engines, and anything out of place. Then they run checklists and agree on what to do if something goes wrong on takeoff.

Now, you’ve probably heard that planes basically fly themselves. Well, that’s only half true. The autopilot does a lot of the work in cruise, but the pilots tell it what to do, watch it the whole time, and take over when they need to. Think of it like cruise control in a car. Very helpful. You still wouldn’t take a nap.

Fun detail. Many airlines prefer that the two pilots eat different meals. Why? Because if one meal is bad, only one pilot gets sick.

So, what does a job like that pay?

Many pilots begin at regional airlines, flying smaller jets under names like United Express or American Eagle. They’re often paid by the flight hour, with a monthly minimum guaranteed. A US government report found first-year first officers at a group of regional airlines averaged about 93 dollars an hour in 2024, up from about 52 dollars in 2021, after adjusting for inflation. At 93 dollars an hour, with 75 hours guaranteed monthly, that’s almost 84,000 dollars a year. In your first year.

At the top, a senior captain flying long-haul jets can earn over 400,000 dollars a year. But that takes years. The US government puts median airline pilot pay at about 232,000 dollars annually, mixing newer pilots with veterans.

So with pay like that, can anyone become an airline pilot?

Mostly, yes. You don’t need to be a genius. Basic math and some physics will help, but what matters more is staying calm, following procedures, communicating clearly, and paying attention to detail.

What about a degree? The big US airlines generally don’t require a four-year degree, though many still prefer one.

And what about glasses? Good news. Glasses and contacts are fine if your corrected vision meets the standards. But airline pilots also need a first-class medical certificate, which checks things like hearing, color vision, heart health, and other conditions. So check your medical eligibility with an FAA-authorized aviation medical examiner before spending serious money on training. You don’t want a surprise after taking out a big loan.

Okay, let’s say you pass. Now what?

Let’s follow Jake. He’s 18, he’s never flown a plane, and he walks into a flight school. An instructor sits beside him in a small training plane and shows him the controls, how to talk to air traffic control, and how to take off and land.

Then, after enough practice, comes the day every pilot remembers. The instructor climbs out, and Jake flies the plane completely alone. That’s his first solo. Nobody beside him to grab the controls. Just him, the runway, and his heartbeat.

From there, Jake climbs a ladder of licenses, and each one solves a real problem.

The first is the private pilot certificate. It proves Jake can fly a small plane safely, navigate, read the weather, and follow the rules. Under the standard US training rules, that takes at least 40 hours of flying, or as few as 35 at certain approved schools. Most students need more. With it, Jake can take friends flying, but he can’t operate flights for hire.

Next problem. What happens if Jake flies into a cloud and can’t see the horizon? Here’s the strange part. His body starts lying to him. Without a clear view outside, his inner ear can tell him he’s level when the plane is turning, or turning when he’s flying straight. So the instrument rating teaches Jake to trust the instruments instead of misleading sensations. That’s harder than it sounds.

Then comes the commercial pilot certificate. Now Jake can qualify for certain paid flying jobs. On the standard US route, he needs at least 250 hours, with some approved programs allowing fewer. He also adds a multi-engine rating, because the passenger jets he wants to fly have more than one engine.

And each major certificate or rating involves examinations, including a practical flying test called a checkride. Picture Jake on the morning of one. He’s practiced every maneuver dozens of times. But today, an examiner watches every decision. Halfway through, the examiner pulls back the power to simulate an engine failure. Jake must pick a place to land, set up the glide, and follow procedures while being graded. That’s what makes flight school hard. Doing something right once is one thing. Doing it reliably under pressure is another.

So, in an intensive training program, Jake could earn his commercial pilot certificate in about a year. Can he finally fly for an airline? Not yet. But before we get to why, there’s something we skipped. Who pays for all of this?

Every hour in the air means paying for the plane, the fuel, and the instructor, plus books and exam fees. One major US flight school advertises 123,995 dollars in tuition to take a student from zero experience through commercial pilot and flight instructor qualifications. And that still doesn’t cover every expense they might face before an airline hires them.

So how do people pay? Savings, family help, loans, scholarships, or military training, which we’ll discuss shortly.

Okay, now, back to Jake. Why can’t he fly for an airline yet? Because of one number. 1,500.

To fly for a US passenger airline, even as a first officer, you generally need an Airline Transport Pilot certificate, or ATP. And the standard requirement is 1,500 hours of flying. That rule followed a regional airline crash in 2009. Congress changed the law, and airlines could no longer put first officers into these jobs with just a commercial pilot certificate.

So let’s do the math. Jake has about 250 hours. He needs 1,500. That’s 1,250 hours still to go.

Why does it matter so much? Because knowing how a plane works isn’t the same as knowing what to do when the weather turns, the radio gets busy, and something doesn’t sound right. That kind of judgment develops through training and real experience.

Take Chesley “Sully” Sullenberger. He flew fighter jets in the Air Force, then joined an airline in 1980. On January 15, 2009, a flock of geese caused both engines of his US Airways jet to lose almost all their thrust shortly after leaving New York’s LaGuardia Airport. He landed it on the Hudson River, and all 155 people on board survived. Later, he told Katie Couric he’d spent 42 years making “small, regular deposits in this bank of experience.” On that day, the balance was big enough for one sudden, huge withdrawal.

Jake isn’t Sully, and 1,500 hours won’t make him Sully. But the example shows why experience matters. Every hour is another chance to build judgment.

So how does Jake fill the gap? There’s more than one way.

One common route is to teach. Jake earns a flight instructor certificate and trains new students. Every eligible hour flying with them counts toward his total. At 80 hours a month, 1,250 hours takes around 16 months. And he’s getting paid.

Other pilots take different roads. The military pays for training, and qualified US military pilots may be eligible for a restricted ATP with as few as 750 hours. In return, they commit to years of service, depending on the branch. Some graduates of approved four-year aviation programs can qualify at 1,000 hours. Airlines also run career pipelines, such as United’s Aviate, Delta’s Propel, and American’s Cadet Academy, where some participants receive a conditional path toward an airline job.

Outside the US, things can look different. In the UK and much of Europe, pilots may join airlines as first officers with a few hundred hours, after commercial and instrument training, airline theory exams, and multi-crew and aircraft-specific training. They build experience toward a full airline transport pilot license while working.

So, Jake hits 1,500 hours. Is he hired? Okay, small problem. Remember how old Jake was when he started? Eighteen. A year of training plus sixteen months of teaching makes him about twenty. In the US, the earliest he can qualify to fly as an airline first officer is twenty-one, with a restricted ATP and all the other required qualifications. So Jake keeps teaching and building experience until he’s old enough.

Becoming a captain takes more. He generally needs to be at least twenty-three, hold an unrestricted ATP, gain qualifying airline experience, pass further evaluations, and wait for a captain position.

But first, Jake has to get hired. That means technical interviews, aptitude tests, sometimes a simulator evaluation, and background and medical checks. Airlines want to know how he handles pressure, and whether he speaks up when something’s wrong.

Timing matters too. In both 2022 and 2023, major US airlines hired more than 12,000 pilots a year. In 2024 and 2025, that dropped to fewer than 5,000 a year. Hiring rises and falls with economic conditions and airline plans, so qualified pilots sometimes have to wait.

And once Jake is hired, he’s back in school. A small training plane is nothing like a Boeing 737, so the airline trains him on the specific type of aircraft he’ll fly, with long days in simulators and another practical evaluation before he begins regular passenger flights.

So, let’s add it up. About a year of intensive training. Sixteen months building hours. More time until he turns twenty-one, then hiring and airline training. If everything goes smoothly, Jake could reach the right seat of a passenger jet in roughly three years. Plenty take longer, especially with part-time training, delays, or a difficult job market.

And like any dream job, this one has trade-offs. Airlines run heavily on seniority, meaning how long you’ve been there. It affects your schedule, home base, aircraft, and vacation choices. New pilots may work weekends, holidays, and early mornings, spend nights in hotels, and face recurring training and medical checks. In the US, pilots at Part 121 airlines must retire from those operations at 65.

So, how hard is it to actually become an airline pilot? Honestly, it’s hard. But it isn’t a mystery, and it isn’t only for geniuses. It takes a passing medical, a ladder of licenses, significant money or a service commitment, lots of flying experience, and patience with timing. Some people find the training hardest. Others struggle with the cost. Others meet the qualifications and still have to wait for the right opportunity. But there’s more than one road in.

And if this is you, a practical first step is to book a discovery flight, a short introductory lesson where an instructor lets you experience the controls. It’s a way to find out whether flying actually excites you before committing to years of training.

So, the next time you see those two pilots walking through the terminal, you’ll know exactly what it took to earn those stripes.
