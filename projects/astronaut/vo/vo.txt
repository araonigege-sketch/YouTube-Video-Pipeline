Hey there! In April 2026, you might have seen the pictures. The Moon, up close and grey. And Earth, a small blue ball hanging in the dark. They came from four people aboard a spacecraft called Orion, on a ten-day trip around the Moon that carried them farther from home than any humans had ever traveled.

One of those four was a Canadian named Jeremy Hansen, a fighter pilot from the Royal Canadian Air Force. And this is the part that surprises people. Hansen had officially been an astronaut since 2009. That’s 17 years. And this was the first time he had ever left Earth.

Wait, what? Seventeen years as an astronaut, and no trip to space?

Yep. And that one fact says a lot about this job. The rocket ride is the rare part. Most of an astronaut’s working life happens right here on the ground: training, testing equipment, and helping the people who actually are up there.

So how do you get picked in the first place? Do you have to be American? A fighter pilot? A genius? And once you finally make it up there, what is life actually like?

In this video, we’ll follow Hansen’s long road to the Moon, meet a farmworker’s son NASA rejected eleven times, count down how thousands of applicants become a handful of astronauts, and find out what a normal day in orbit really looks like. Let’s get started.

First, a question that matters for a lot of you watching. Can you apply to NASA?

Well, only if you’re a US citizen. It’s the very first line of NASA’s requirements. Dual citizens count. Everyone else? Nope.

But that doesn’t close the door. Many countries pick their own astronauts. Hansen applied to the Canadian Space Agency. Europe has the European Space Agency. Japan, India, China, and the United Arab Emirates train astronauts too. So before your grades or your fitness, the first thing that shapes this career is your passport. It decides which agency you can even apply to.

And those agencies don’t hire every year. When Hansen was picked in 2009, it was only the third time Canada had ever recruited astronauts. And when Europe chose its newest class in 2022, it was the first new group it had picked since 2009. For most people on Earth, the window opens rarely.

Okay, say the window opens. What do you need? NASA publishes its list in detail, so let’s use it as our example.

A master’s degree in a science, technology, engineering, or math field. A medical degree, or test pilot school, can also count. Then at least three years of real professional work after your degree. Or, if you’re a pilot, 750 hours as pilot in command of a high-performance jet can count instead. Other types of aircraft have different requirements. And you must pass NASA’s long-duration spaceflight physical, with no medical waivers.

Wait, so you don’t have to be a pilot? Nope. Engineers, scientists, and doctors get picked too. You don’t need a military background either. There’s no age limit. And glasses? Fine. NASA has even allowed laser eye surgery since 2007.

Sounds doable? Well, that list only gets you through the front door. Now comes the countdown.

In NASA’s 2021 selection, more than 12,000 people applied. Experts reviewed the files of about 3,000 of them. Then 120 were invited to Houston, where each one sat alone in front of the astronaut selection board. Then 30 came back for a second round. And at the very end, NASA picked 10.

Twelve thousand people. Ten seats.

In 2025, it happened again. More than 8,000 applicants, 10 picked. That’s about one in every 800.

Hansen’s round was even tighter. Canada’s 2009 search drew 5,351 applicants. It picked two. And Europe’s 2021 call drew more than 22,500 people. It picked 17, and only five of them got full-time astronaut jobs. Most of the rest went into a reserve, people who passed everything but still had to wait for a chance to fly.

So if almost everyone in that final room is brilliant, what actually decides who gets in?

Well, look at that second round. Candidates spend about a week with NASA. They go through medical tests. They get measured, to make sure they physically fit in the spacecraft seats. And they do team exercises designed to show how they behave around other people.

Because NASA isn’t just asking whether you can solve a hard problem. It needs to know whether you can solve it while exhausted, under pressure, and floating next to someone you were arguing with five minutes ago. On a space station, you can’t step outside to cool off.

And plenty of excellent people don’t get picked, at least not at first. Nobody shows that better than José Hernández.

Hernández grew up in a family of migrant farmworkers in California, moving from field to field to harvest crops. He didn’t learn English until he was 12. He became an engineer anyway, and after graduate school, he applied to NASA.

Rejected.

He applied again. Rejected again.

In total, NASA turned him down eleven times. Eleven! On his twelfth try, in 2004, at 41 years old, he was finally selected.

And then? He didn’t fly until 2009. Five more years. And believe it or not, by astronaut standards, that wasn’t even a long wait.

So what happens after you’re picked? Congratulations! You’re… not an astronaut yet. You’re an astronaut candidate.

For nearly two years, candidates learn spacecraft systems, fly T-38 jets, and study robotics, geology, and survival. Oh, and during the first month, you have to pass a swimming test. Imagine beating 12,000 applicants, then sweating about the pool.

Speaking of pools. Where do you practice a spacewalk when you’ve never been to space? Apparently, in the water. NASA’s Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory holds 6.2 million gallons, with full-size models of space station modules sitting on the bottom. Floating in a pool isn’t the same as being weightless, but it’s one of the best ways to rehearse the movements before you try them for real.

Hansen got through all of it, and graduated in 2011. And then came the part nobody puts on a poster.

Graduating doesn’t mean you fly. NASA’s own job posting says that after training, you become eligible for a spaceflight assignment. Eligible is the key word. Until a mission comes, you work on the ground, supporting the astronauts in space and advising on new spacecraft. And once you do get assigned, there’s a whole new round of training for that specific mission.

There aren’t many seats, either. In late 2026, NASA had just 32 active astronauts, and crewed missions have only a handful of places available.

For Hansen, the ground years were long. In 2017, he became the first non-American to lead a new NASA astronaut class through its training. Think about that. He was teaching other people how to go to space, and he still hadn’t been. Meanwhile, the man picked alongside him in 2009, David Saint-Jacques, flew to the space station at the end of 2018.

Hansen kept waiting. Then in 2023, he was finally named to Artemis II. And then the launch slipped. It was expected in 2024. Then 2025. Then, on April 1, 2026, he launched. He had been 33 when Canada picked him. He was 50 when he left the ground. And he became the first person who isn’t American to fly around the Moon.

So yes, it’s a dream job. It’s also a test of patience most people would fail.

Now, Hansen’s trip around the Moon lasted about ten days. But what if your mission takes you to the International Space Station, where astronauts can spend months living and working in orbit? Is it everything you imagined?

Well, yes. And no.

The view is real. The station circles Earth about every 90 minutes, so the crew sees a sunrise or a sunset roughly every 45. But it’s not a sightseeing trip. It’s a workplace. On a normal day, you might run science experiments, check equipment, or fix something that broke.

And then there’s the gym. NASA schedules astronauts for about two and a half hours of exercise a day, including setting up and packing away. Because in weightlessness, your muscles and bones barely have to work. And no, floating around all day doesn’t count as your workout.

Everyday life gets weird too. You sleep in a sleeping bag strapped to the wall, ideally near a fan, so fresh air keeps moving around your face. You wash with damp washcloths and rinseless shampoo. Your body fluids drift up toward your head, which can feel like a constant head cold.

And the toilet? It works with airflow and suction instead of gravity. As for the urine, it gets filtered, processed, and recycled. Into drinking water. With its upgraded system, the station has demonstrated that it can recover 98 percent of its water, sweat included. As NASA astronaut Jessica Meir put it, today’s coffee is tomorrow’s coffee.

Then there’s what space does to your body over time. NASA says that on four-to-six month missions, astronauts lose on average between 1 and 1.5 percent of their bone density every month. Canada’s space agency compares a six-month stay to almost 20 years of bone loss on Earth. Exercise helps reduce the loss, and astronauts can recover some of it after they return. But not all of it necessarily comes back.

And sometimes, space simply has its own plans. In June 2024, NASA astronauts Butch Wilmore and Suni Williams launched on Boeing’s new Starliner spacecraft for a test flight to the station. The trip was supposed to last about eight days.

But Starliner ran into problems. So it flew home without them, and the two astronauts stayed on the station. They finally splashed down in March 2025. After 286 days. Months away from family, in the same small group, with no way to leave early. And during that extra time, Williams still went outside on spacewalks. That calm, get-on-with-it attitude is exactly what those interview weeks are looking for.

And the risks are real. NASA lost two space shuttles, Challenger in 1986 and Columbia in 2003. Fourteen astronauts died. Every person who straps into a rocket knows that history.

Alright. So what does this job actually pay?

At NASA, astronauts are government employees. When NASA opened applications again in November 2026, it listed a salary of about 164,000 dollars a year. That’s very good money. But it’s not superstar money, for a job where your commute is a rocket.

And this is a fun contrast with our airline pilot episode. Remember how flight school there could cost more than 120,000 dollars? NASA astronaut candidates don’t pay for astronaut school. They get paid while they train. The expensive part is everything before: the degrees, and the years of building a career good enough to apply.

And there’s one more twist. The route to space is starting to change.

Remember NASA’s 2025 class of 10? One of them, Anna Menon, had already been to space. Menon was a biomedical engineer who worked in NASA’s Mission Control, then at SpaceX helping run crew operations. In September 2024, she flew on Polaris Dawn, a private SpaceX mission, as its medical officer. A year later, NASA picked her for astronaut training.

Wait, she went to space before NASA selected her? Yep. She’s the first NASA astronaut candidate who had already flown to orbit.

And it’s not just an American story. In June 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla, an Indian Air Force test pilot, flew to the space station on Axiom Mission 4, a flight run by a private company. He became the first Indian ever to board the station, and only the second Indian in space, 41 years after the first.

Both stories have something in common, though. Menon had spent years in mission operations before she ever flew. Shukla had already been chosen by India’s space agency. Buying a ticket as a tourist is a different thing entirely. Private flights are opening new doors, but for a career, someone still has to pick you.

So, what’s the reality? Becoming an astronaut takes a serious degree, years of real work, a body that passes every medical test, a passport that matches an agency, and a selection board that picks you out of thousands. Then it takes two years of training, and a patience most jobs never ask for.

If you want a real shot, don’t chase the title alone. Get excellent at something real first, like engineering, medicine, science, or flying. Look after your health. Learn to stay calm and useful in a team when things go wrong. Watch for your country’s next call. And if you don’t get in, remember José Hernández, and apply again.

Because Jeremy Hansen waited 17 years for his first launch. And when it finally came, he and his crewmates went farther from Earth than any humans in history.
