Hey there! Look at the phone in your hand, or the screen you’re watching this on. Somewhere, one person sits at the very top of the company that made it. One person who answers for every chip, every update, and every dollar. That person is the CEO.

Now, here’s something weird. You could become a CEO by tomorrow afternoon. Register a small company, print some business cards, and write “CEO” under your name. There’s no exam to pass and no license to earn. Congratulations!

But try becoming CEO of the company that made your phone. Go to its careers page. You’ll find jobs for engineers, designers, accountants, and cleaners. You will never find a posting that says “Chief Executive Officer. Apply here.”

Because at the world’s biggest companies, nobody gets the top job by sending in an application. You get chosen. And, as we’ll see, you can get unchosen.

So who does the choosing? What are they looking for? Do you need a famous degree, or rich parents? And how long does it take?

In this video, we’ll follow real people who reached the top from a warehouse floor, a garden center, a design studio, and a booth in a 24-hour diner. We’ll find out who picks a CEO, what the job really pays, and why getting the chair might not even be the hardest part. Let’s get started.

First, what does a CEO actually do?

Well, CEO stands for chief executive officer. It’s the highest-ranking manager in a company. The CEO sets the direction, decides where the money goes, and picks the team that runs everything else.

But the job changes completely with size. If you run a five-person bakery, you’re the CEO, and also the accountant, the hiring manager, and the person who mops the floor at closing time. Run a company with 100,000 workers, and you’ll never meet most of them. Same title. Very different job.

So the CEO is the boss of everything, right? Well, at big companies, nope. Above the CEO sits the board of directors, a small group chosen to look after the shareholders, the people who own the company. The board has one power that matters more than any other. It hires the CEO. And it can fire the CEO.

Yep. The person at the top still has a boss. Actually, about ten of them.

And the seats are incredibly rare. One yearly study tracks CEO changes at about 1,500 of America’s biggest public companies. Among the top 500 of them, only 59 got a new CEO in all of 2025. Fifty-nine seats, in a country with millions of managers.

So let’s say you’re a senior executive. Your results are excellent. Everyone agrees you’re a strong leader. Do you become CEO next?

Well, not necessarily. Your company already has one.

Picture forty people inside a company who are genuinely good enough for the top job. Thirty-nine of them will not get it. They didn’t fail. There was only one chair.

So how do people actually reach it? There are three main doors.

The first door is the one most CEOs walk through. You rise inside the company. In that same 2025 study, about 60 percent of new CEOs were promoted from within.

And some of those stories are wild. Look at Walmart, a company with about 2.1 million workers around the world.

In 1984, a teenager named Doug McMillon took an hourly job in a Walmart warehouse, picking orders and unloading trucks. Thirty years later, in 2014, he became CEO of the entire company.

Then in 2026, when McMillon retired, Walmart’s board picked his replacement. John Furner. And guess where Furner started? As an hourly worker in a Walmart garden center in Bentonville, Arkansas.

So one of the biggest employers on Earth has now had two CEOs in a row who started out being paid by the hour.

Wait, so anyone can just work their way up? Well, not exactly. Because nobody really gets promoted to CEO. You get tested for it.

Think of a future CEO’s career as a series of tests, each one more expensive to fail. Can you manage five people? Good, here’s a department. Handled that? Here’s a whole region. Now a struggling division. Now a crisis.

And the skills being tested keep changing. Early on, you win by being the best at one thing. But further up, the board wants someone who understands how sales, factories, money, people, and risk all fit together. Depth wins at the bottom. Breadth wins in the middle. At the top, what wins is seeing how everything connects.

People in this world call it “P and L responsibility.” P and L means profit and loss. It means you answer for a whole chunk of the business: the money coming in, the money going out, and what’s left at the end.

Look at Furner’s path. Store manager. District manager. Buyer. Then he moved to Shenzhen to run merchandising and marketing for Walmart China. Then he ran Sam’s Club. Then all of Walmart in the US. Every step handed him a bigger, messier test.

So what about school? Do you need Harvard? Well, it doesn’t hurt. But when one professor checked the CEOs of America’s 100 biggest companies in 2023, fewer than 12 percent had gone to an Ivy League school for their first degree. Furner studied at the University of Arkansas. A bachelor’s degree is typical, and an MBA can help. But there’s no degree that comes with a CEO job attached. If there were, business schools would charge a lot more.

Okay. Now let’s flip it around. Here’s a made-up example. You’re on the board.

Your company is one of the most valuable on Earth. Your CEO is stepping down. You’re choosing between two kinds of candidate.

Candidate A is a classic operator. He runs the supply chains and operations, the machine that keeps everything moving.

Candidate B is an engineer. He’s spent his career building the products, down to the materials they’re made of. But he’s never been the company’s operations chief.

Who do you pick?

Well, Apple never said it had a shortlist like that. But its real choices show the idea. The last time it changed CEO, in 2011, it didn’t look for another Steve Jobs. It picked Tim Cook, an operations man. Then in April 2026, Apple announced its next CEO. John Ternus, an engineer who joined Apple’s product design team in 2001 and spent the next 25 years building its hardware, from the iPad to the AirPods. The board approved him unanimously, after what Apple called a long-term succession planning process.

So boards don’t pick the best executive in the building. They pick the one they believe fits what comes next. Which means you can do everything right and still not get picked.

So what happens then? That’s the second door. Leave, and become CEO somewhere else.

Jamie Dimon knows this door well. He spent about 15 years working alongside his mentor, building what became Citigroup. Dimon rose to president. He looked like the heir. Then in 1998, his mentor asked him to resign. Fired, by his own mentor.

In 2000, Dimon became CEO of Bank One, a Chicago bank that lost 511 million dollars that year. About three years later, it reported a record profit of 3.5 billion dollars. Then JPMorgan Chase bought Bank One, and at the end of 2005, Dimon became CEO of one of the biggest banks in the world.

And setbacks like his are surprisingly normal. One ten-year study of more than 2,000 CEO candidates found that 45 percent had suffered at least one major career blowup, like losing a job. And most of those people still got the top job.

And then there’s the third door. You skip the line completely, and start your own company. Nobody picks you. You pick yourself.

Jensen Huang did exactly that. As a teenager, he washed dishes and bussed tables at Denny’s, an American diner chain. Then in 1993, at 30 years old, he sat in a Denny’s booth in San Jose with two engineers and planned a company that would build chips for realistic 3D graphics. That company was Nvidia.

Now, the title was the easy part. Your business card doesn’t find customers or pay your engineers. Huang had to actually build the thing. And he did. He ran Nvidia for 33 years, and its chips went on to power the AI boom, making it one of the most valuable companies on Earth.

Founder. CEO. The face of the whole company. Untouchable, right?

Nope. Once a company grows and takes money from investors, it gets a board too. And that board can remove you, even if you started it.

In November 2026, reports about Huang’s personal life, and what they might mean for US national security, shook the company. Days later, Nvidia announced he had resigned, effective immediately. CNBC reported that the board had decided he had to go. Thirty-three years in the chair, ended by one board decision. And he isn’t the first. Steve Jobs was pushed out of Apple, the company he co-founded, back in 1985.

When he left, the board put Nvidia’s finance chief in charge temporarily and started searching inside and outside the company for a permanent CEO. One of the most watched CEO seats in the world was suddenly open. And there was no job ad.

But say you make it. You finally get the chair. What exactly are you being trusted with?

Picture an ordinary Monday. You have enough money for one big project, not two. Your finance chief wants the one that brings in profit next year. Your head of product wants the one that could matter in ten years. Both have good reasons. The board wants results. So who makes the call? You do. And if it goes wrong, that’s on you too. Most of the job is decisions like that, with no perfect answer.

And sometimes, the job hands you a problem you never made. Mary Barra started at General Motors in 1980 as an 18-year-old student. Over the next three decades, she worked in engineering, ran a car plant, led human resources, and ran product development. In January 2014, she became CEO. Thirty-four years of tests, passed.

A few months later, she was in front of the US Congress. GM had used a faulty ignition switch in some of its small cars, and the company had taken about ten years to recall them. At the time, the switch was linked to 13 deaths. GM’s own compensation fund later paid claims for 124. The failures began long before Barra was CEO. But she was the one answering for them.

That’s the job. You inherit problems you didn’t create, and you still have to fix them.

Alright. So why would anyone go through all this? Let’s talk money.

In the US, the median chief executive earned about 214,000 dollars in 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. That counts a huge range of organizations, many of them small. At America’s biggest public companies, it’s a different world. According to an Associated Press survey of S&P 500 companies, the typical CEO pay package was about 17.7 million dollars.

But a big chunk of that number arrives as company stock, often stock they can’t cash in for years, and sometimes never, unless the company hits certain targets.

And the chair isn’t as safe as it looks. Back in that yearly study of 1,500 companies, the CEOs who stepped down in 2025 had lasted about 8.5 years on average. And more than a third of them were gone within their first five.

So how long does the whole journey take? The new CEOs that year were about 54 years old on average. And 84 percent had never been a CEO before. For most of them, the chair is the end of a 25 to 30 year career.

And yes, luck plays a part. The right boss. The right division at the right moment. A seat that opens when you’re ready. We mostly hear from the people the dice landed for, not from the ones who worked just as hard and stopped one level short.

Still, the people who make it tend to ask for bigger responsibility, not bigger titles. They move across the business instead of staying in one lane. And over many years, they earn the trust of the people who will one day decide.

So how hard is it really to become a CEO? Giving yourself the CEO title can take an afternoon. Building a company that deserves it can take a lifetime. And running one of the world’s biggest companies usually takes decades, with no guarantee the chair goes to you, even if you’re good enough.

Because at that level, being qualified isn’t enough. In the end, the biggest promotion of your life happens in a room you’re not in. A few people look at everything you’ve done and ask one question: do we trust this person with the whole company?

If you want to give yourself a real shot, the first step isn’t a fancy title. Raise your hand for the work nobody else wants. Run the small project. Fix the struggling team. Learn exactly how your company makes money.
