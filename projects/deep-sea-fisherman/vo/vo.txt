Hey there! You’ve probably seen this on TV. A fishing boat climbs a wave the size of a house in the middle of the Bering Sea. Ice covers the railings. A crew in orange rain gear hauls a giant steel cage out of the water, and it’s packed with crab. Every person on that deck is chasing the same thing: a share of that catch.

And here’s the strange part. While they’re out there, most of them don’t know exactly how much they’ll be paid until the catch is sold.

It looks wild, right? But it also looks like a job almost anyone could walk into. No degree. No exam. No years of school.

So, what does it actually take to become a deep-sea fisherman? Do you need special qualifications? How does a total beginner get hired? How dangerous is it, really? And is the money worth it?

In this video, we’ll follow one beginner from the dock to his first trip, and find out what it really takes. Let’s get started.

So, what is a deep-sea fisherman? Well, it’s someone who works on a commercial fishing boat that travels far offshore, often staying out for days or weeks at a time. And no, they’re not underwater, and they’re definitely not sitting there with a fishing rod.

And there are different kinds of boats. Trawlers drag huge nets through the water. Longliners set out miles of line with thousands of baited hooks. Pot boats drop heavy steel traps called pots onto the seafloor and come back to haul them up. The biggest trawlers are floating factories, about as long as a football field, where the fish are cleaned, packed, and frozen before the ship ever returns to port.

You’ll find this work all over the world, from Gulf of Mexico shrimp boats to North Atlantic trawlers. Pay, rules, and conditions change a lot from one fishery and country to the next. But Alaska is one of the best-documented places to see how it works, so that’s where most of our examples come from.

And the job isn’t just catching fish. Crews also sort the catch, repair gear, keep machinery running, and clean the boat. On a commercial fishing boat, you’re part factory worker, part sailor, and part mechanic.

Every boat has a ranking. The captain decides where to fish. The engineer keeps the engines alive. The deck boss runs the crew on deck. Then come the deckhands. And at the very bottom is the newest person on board, the one with no experience. In fishing, that person has a name. The greenhorn.

So, let’s say a guy named Daniel wants this life. He’s 23, reasonably fit, and has never worked on a boat. What does he need?

Well, on paper, surprisingly little. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics says fishing workers usually learn on the job, with no formal education required. If Daniel works on deck in an Alaska fishery, he’ll need a commercial crewmember license from the state, which costs 60 dollars a year for residents and 220 for everyone else. Job ads also ask for things like lifting 50 pounds over and over, working long hours in cold, wet conditions, and passing a drug test.

Wait, that’s it? A license and a strong back? Then why is getting hired so hard?

Well, because nobody wants to bet on a stranger. Picture a small crew, far from land, in dangerous water. If one new person can’t keep up or gets hurt, everyone suffers, including in their paychecks. So captains prefer people they know, or people someone they trust can vouch for. And that creates a trap. You need experience to be trusted on a boat. But how do you get experience if nobody trusts you enough to take you out?

There are a few ways around it.

The first way is the factory. Big factory-trawler companies, mostly based in Seattle, hire entry-level processors with no fishing experience. The second way is summer salmon. In Bristol Bay, Alaska, the world’s biggest wild sockeye salmon run draws well over a thousand boats every summer. Those drift boats are limited to 32 feet, and many captains hire one or two deckhands for a few intense weeks. And the third way is old school: walking the docks in places like Dutch Harbor, Kodiak, or Seattle’s Fishermen’s Terminal, asking boat after boat if they need a hand. Most say no. All it takes is one yes.

Daniel goes the factory route. He applies to a factory-trawler company in Seattle, gets hired as an entry-level processor, and signs his first contract. And now he’s about to learn that getting hired was the easy part.

The ship loads fuel, food, and supplies, then heads out, sometimes traveling for a day or more just to reach the fishing grounds. The captain uses sonar, weather reports, and experience to decide where to drop the net.

Then the real work starts. The trawl net is hauled up the back of the ship, heavy with fish, and the catch slides down into holding tanks below deck. That’s where Daniel works. Fish come down the line, and the crew sorts them, cuts them, packs them into trays, and loads them into freezers. Then the next haul arrives, and it starts again. Over and over, for hours, at three in the morning as easily as three in the afternoon.

And the schedule? Yeah, forget nine to five. Job postings on factory ships often list 16-hour shifts, seven days a week, on contracts of around two months. When your shift ends, you eat, you sleep in a small bunk on a moving ship, and you wake up to do it again. There’s no commute home. You already live at work.

That’s Daniel’s first big surprise. The hardest part usually isn’t one terrifying moment. It’s the exhaustion that piles up, day after day.

And on top of all that, Daniel has a lot to learn, fast. How to move around a rolling deck. How to keep clear of gear and lines under tension. Hand signals, because machinery is loud. And what to do in an emergency.

Here’s a quick test. It’s the middle of the night. An alarm goes off. The ship is leaning hard to one side, and the crew has to abandon ship. Can you get into a full-body survival suit in under a minute?

Honestly, most people can’t on their first try. But that’s the goal fishermen train for. These thick, waterproof suits, nicknamed “Gumby suits,” can keep you alive in freezing water. Safety trainers teach crews to get into them in sixty seconds or less, and many crews practice in regular drills. In the dark, on a tilting deck, that’s much harder than it sounds.

Which brings us to the big question. How dangerous is this job, really?

Here’s the honest answer. It’s still one of the most dangerous jobs in America. In 2024, fishing and hunting workers died on the job at a rate about 27 times higher than the average US worker, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The biggest dangers are boats sinking or capsizing, people falling overboard, and injuries from the gear on deck.

And what makes it dangerous isn’t always a giant storm. It’s ordinary things combined. A wet deck plus a sudden wave. Heavy gear plus a tired crew. Freezing water plus being hours from help.

But here’s what TV doesn’t always tell you. It used to be much worse. In the 1990s, the Bering Sea crab fleet was the most dangerous fishery in the US, losing about eight people a year on average. Then in 1999, the Coast Guard started checking crab boats at the dock to make sure they weren’t overloaded. And in 2005, the crab fishery switched from a mad race for crab to a quota system, so boats no longer had to fish nonstop through storms to beat everyone else. Since then, deaths in the crab fleet have dropped to less than one a year on average.

So the danger is real, but good boats and good crews manage it every day. Which leaves one question. Is the money worth it?

Well, remember how Daniel might not know his paycheck? Here’s why. On a lot of fishing boats, there’s no fixed salary. Instead, you get a crew share, which is a percentage of what the catch sells for.

Let’s say a boat sells a trip’s catch for 100,000 dollars. Does the crew split all 100,000? Nope. First, shared costs like fuel, bait, and food come off the top. The boat and its owner take their cut. What’s left is divided into shares, and experienced crew usually get bigger shares than greenhorns. Every boat’s deal is different, so this is just an example, not a fixed formula.

Now, what does that look like in real life? Well, Glacier Fish Company, which runs factory trawlers out of Alaska, advertises that its entry-level processors average between 3,600 and 5,000 dollars per fishing trip, depending on what the catch is worth. And since you live on the ship, room and food are usually covered.

But your paycheck also depends on the ocean. A few years ago, the Bering Sea snow crab population crashed, and Alaska shut the fishery down for two seasons. It reopened in late 2024, and the next season’s limit nearly doubled. Then for the 2026 to 2027 season, it dropped again by about 45 percent. So the same hard work can pay very differently from one year to the next. Your effort matters, but your effort alone doesn’t decide your paycheck.

And money isn’t the only price. Daniel will miss birthdays, holidays, and ordinary evenings at home. He’ll share tight spaces with the same people for weeks, often without phone signal.

Yet plenty of people love it. The friendships on a good crew can last a lifetime. There’s real pride in hard, physical work, and in coming home after a great season. For some people, being that far from a city sounds like a nightmare. For others, that’s exactly the point.

So, can Daniel turn this into a real career? He can, but there’s no fixed timetable. Each contract builds sea time, references, and people who can vouch for him. That’s how a processor can eventually land a spot on deck, maybe on a crab boat, where the work looks completely different. There, the crew baits steel pots, drops them to the seafloor, hauls them back up with a hydraulic winch, and sorts the catch. Only the big males can be kept. Females and small crab? Sorry, back you go.

From deckhand, the paths split. Some move up to deck boss, running the crew on deck. Others take a technical path toward engineer, learning to keep the engines and machinery running. The best deck crew may get trained to run the boat while the captain sleeps, which is called a relief skipper. And some eventually become captains themselves, though running bigger vessels can require formal Coast Guard licensing too.

Take Sig Hansen. He’s the captain of the Northwestern, one of the best-known crab boats in the Bering Sea. His father was a Norwegian fisherman who helped pioneer Alaska’s crab fishing. Sig started fishing at 14 on the family boat and spent summers on a salmon boat in Bristol Bay. By 22, he was a relief skipper. By 24, he was running the Northwestern full time.

But wait, notice something. Sig had a family boat. Most people don’t, which is exactly why Daniel’s first factory contract matters so much. It’s how outsiders build the trust that insiders are born with. And if Daniel decides he doesn’t want decades at sea, skills like machinery repair, refrigeration, and fish processing can carry him into other parts of the seafood and maritime world.

So, what does it take to be a deep-sea fisherman? Not a degree. It takes endurance, discipline, a strong stomach, the ability to work with others, and a healthy respect for the ocean. So yeah, on paper, getting in is surprisingly simple. Getting hired, and proving yourself, is a whole other story. And the real test is whether you still want to go back out after your first trip.

If this sounds like you, start with the fishing industry where you want to work. Look for legitimate companies hiring entry-level processors or deckhands. In the US, the State of Alaska runs a seafood jobs page. And before you accept any spot, make sure you understand exactly how your share will be paid.

So, the next time you see a crab boat climbing a wave on TV, you’ll know what it took for every person on that deck to get there.
