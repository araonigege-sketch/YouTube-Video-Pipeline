First, why I'm leaving bleach out of it. Bleach is what most of us reach for, because it works fast on the eye. Spray it, and the line goes lighter. And bleach does have its uses. It can lighten stains and kill some of what's growing on the surface. But that doesn't mean it's removed the soap scum, oils, and grime underneath. Those can stay right where they were, ready to collect more dirt. Used week after week, harsh bleach cleaners can also wear down some of the sealers protecting your grout, discolor certain grout finishes, and fill a small bathroom with irritating fumes. You end up working harder without solving the reason the grout keeps getting dirty.

To see why, you need to know what grout actually is. Your tile, if it's glazed ceramic or porcelain, has a hard, smooth face that shrugs off most of what lands on it. Ordinary cement grout is different. It's full of tiny connected pores, a little like a very hard sponge. Water gets in. So does whatever the water is carrying.

And three things keep feeding it. Everyday grime, the dirt from your feet and the oils from your skin. Residue, from soap and from cleaner that never got rinsed off, which dries into a dull film that grabs fresh dirt. And moisture, because grout that stays damp is where the dark spots and mildew often show up first. Three different causes, and none of them gets fixed by scrubbing harder. Aggressive brushes and harsh scrubbing can wear the grout down and roughen it, so it catches even more. Acids like vinegar are no better as a regular cleaner, because repeated use can damage cement grout.

Here's the line I want you to keep: a lot of grout gets dirty again not because you cleaned it badly, but because you cleaned it and left it just as vulnerable as before.

So, the reset. This is the clean that gets your grout ready to test and protect. I'm working on ordinary glazed tile with ordinary cement grout, which you'll find in plenty of bathrooms. If yours is natural stone or a specialty grout, hang on, I'll get to you.

On a floor, sweep or vacuum before anything gets wet. Grit left on the tile just gets dragged around by your brush and ground into the lines.

For grout that's mostly dull and grimy, warm water and a few drops of dish soap, or a pH-neutral tile cleaner, will do more than you'd think. For heavier buildup, the kind left by months of soap and body oils, use an alkaline cleaner specifically approved for your tile and grout. Those cleaners are good at breaking down greasy residue, but they're not all suitable for every sealer or tile finish. So check the label, follow the dilution instructions, and test a hidden corner first. And gloves on if the product calls for them.

Work in a patch about the size of a doormat. Apply your cleaning solution and give it the time the label recommends so it can soften what's in there. Then scrub along the lines with a nylon grout brush, short strokes, steady pressure. Leave the wire brushes in the garage. They scratch tile and chew up grout, and chewed-up grout gets dirty faster than ever.

Now the step that matters more than the scrubbing. When you scrub, all that grime ends up floating in a puddle of dirty water. Leave it to dry, and some of it settles right back into the grout you just cleaned. So pick it up. Sop it with an old towel or run a wet vac over it, then rinse with clean water and pick that up too. Keep going until the water you lift looks clear. I call it the Lift-Out, and it does double duty, because it also takes away cleaner residue, and residue is part of what turned the grout gray in the first place. Dirt you loosen and leave behind is dirt you'll be cleaning twice.

Look at that patch next to one I haven't touched. That's what clean looks like. If some lines are stained right through and the cleaning didn't move them, that's what the cling paste from the earlier video is for, provided it's suitable for your grout. I won't make you sit through it twice.

But clean only gets you halfway. Now we find out whether that grout has the protection it needs.

The grout has to be completely dry for this, and that takes longer than it looks. Give it enough time to dry thoroughly. In a damp bathroom, that may mean a day or two. Then put a small drop of clean water on a grout line and watch how it behaves over the next few minutes.

If the water soaks in quickly and leaves a dark patch that hangs around, that suggests your grout is absorbing moisture and may need protection. If the water stays mostly on the surface and the grout barely changes color, its existing protection may still be doing its job. And if you're not sure, if it darkens a little and fades, treat that as a clue rather than a verdict. Some good sealers still let the very surface get wet, so you're not hunting for a perfect bead. You're watching for signs of absorption. That's the Drop Test. For a more definite answer, follow the testing guidance for your particular grout or sealer.

Remember those two lines from the start? They looked identical. But one absorbed that water much faster than the other. The water showed us a difference our eyes couldn't see.

If yours soaked the water in quickly, and you've confirmed it's ordinary cement grout that needs sealing, here's the step most people skip.

Seal it.

Not every grout needs it. Epoxy grout, for example, absorbs very little water, and most epoxy grouts don't need sealing at all. You can't always identify the type just by looking, so check what was installed if you're unsure. This next step is for cement grout that needs additional protection.

What you want is a clear penetrating grout sealer suitable for your installation. Penetrating means it works below the surface to help the grout resist moisture and staining. Water and the dirt it carries have a harder time soaking in, which gives you a better chance of wiping them away before they settle. Here's what it isn't, though. It isn't a force field. Sealed grout can still get dirty and stained. It just has better resistance. And it won't waterproof your shower. That job belongs to the waterproofing system behind the tile.

The label on your bottle is the boss here, because drying times, coats, and cure times vary a lot between products. But the shape of the job is the same. Make sure the grout is clean, sound, and fully dry. Try the sealer on a small hidden spot first, to make sure it doesn't darken your grout or mark your tile. Then work in small sections, putting it on the grout lines with the applicator the bottle calls for. Wipe off any excess before it dries into a haze, following the product instructions. And keep water and feet off it for as long as the label says.

Once the sealer has fully cured, we can compare again. Same kind of grout, same drop of water, but this time one section has been treated and the other hasn't. Watch how much each one absorbs. The treated section should show better resistance if the sealer has done its job. It might still darken slightly, depending on the product, but the difference can be revealing.

Now the habits, because even the best sealer won't save grout that lives soaking wet.

After a shower, take the standing water off. A squeegee or an old towel down the walls takes very little time. Run the bathroom fan while you shower and for a while afterward, or open a window if that's how your bathroom ventilates. Mold needs moisture, so controlling the damp is a big part of keeping it away.

Once a week, a quick clean with that pH-neutral cleaner or a little dish soap in warm water, and a soft brush or cloth. Rinse it, or wipe it down with clean water. You're getting the fresh soap and grime off before it settles, which is a lot easier than scrubbing the floor on your knees every Saturday.

On floors, watch your mop. One bucket for the whole house means that by the last room you're mopping with dirty water and pushing it into the grout lines. Change the water when it clouds, and wring the mop out properly.

And every so often, check how the grout handles water, especially in the shower and the busiest stretch of floor. If it starts absorbing water much more readily than it used to, check the sealer manufacturer's guidance and whether it's time for another treatment. Don't just keep adding coats because a calendar says so.

How long is months, really? That depends on your water, your traffic, your grout, the protection you've used, and how damp your bathroom stays. But clean it right, seal it if it needs it, and keep excess moisture under control, and you give yourself a much better chance of avoiding that deep clean every few weeks. That's the real win.

Now, a few situations where none of this will be enough, and I'd rather tell you than watch you scrub.

If the grout is still discolored after a proper clean, the color itself may have changed, or the stain went deeper than cleaning can reach. A grout colorant designed for your type of grout can sometimes even it out and give it a fresh appearance, and that may be a better answer than reaching for something stronger.

If dark patches keep coming back in the same spot, especially with a musty smell, that may not be a cleaning problem anymore. Something could be keeping that area wet, a leak, failed caulk, or poor ventilation, and grout sealer won't fix the source of that moisture. Find the water first.

If the grout is cracking, crumbling, or missing, sealer can't hold it together. That section may need repair or regrouting, but you also need to find out why it failed. And check the caulk where the walls meet the tub or the floor. When those joints fail, water can get into places it shouldn't.

And if your tile is natural stone, marble, limestone, or travertine, its care is its own job. Keep acidic cleaners off vulnerable stone, and follow the stone and grout manufacturer's cleaning and sealing guidance.
