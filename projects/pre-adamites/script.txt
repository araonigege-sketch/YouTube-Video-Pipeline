Who Were the Humans Before Adam and Eve?
In 1655, a book appeared in Amsterdam that was burned in Paris almost as soon as it arrived. The next year, its author was arrested in Brussels. And before long, he was in Rome, renouncing it in person before Pope Alexander the Seventh.
His crime? Asking a question almost every child asks in Sunday school.
If Adam and Eve were the first people on earth, and they had two sons, and one killed the other, then who was Cain afraid of? Who did he marry? And who lived in the city he built?
The author’s name was Isaac La Peyrère, and his answer was: there must have been people before Adam. That got him into deep trouble.
Three and a half centuries later, science tells us the earth really was home to ancient humans, generations upon generations of them, stretching back hundreds of thousands of years. People who buried their dead, painted on cave walls, and built great monuments long before the date tradition gave for the first man.
So who were these people? And where do Adam and Eve, the first man and woman of the Bible and the Qur’an, fit into their story?
This isn’t a side question for believers. For Christians, Adam is where sin entered the world, and Paul builds a central part of his account of salvation on that. For Muslims, Adam is the first human and the first prophet, the one God taught the names of all things, in Surah 2, verse 31. For Jews, he’s where the Torah’s human story begins. If there were people before him, or alongside him, every one of those traditions has to ask what Adam actually was.
To find out, we’ll go to the scriptures first, then to the bones and the DNA, and then to the long line of Jewish, Christian and Muslim thinkers who’ve tried to bring the two together, from ancient rabbis to scholars writing in the last few years.
The story is familiar. In Genesis, God forms the man from the dust of the ground and breathes life into him. He places him in a garden, and makes the woman from his side. The man later names her Eve, and Genesis explains that she would be the mother of all who live. After they eat the forbidden fruit, they’re driven out of Eden, and they have two sons, Cain and Abel. Cain kills his brother, and God sends him away as a wanderer.
And then Cain says something strange.
“You have banished me from the land and from your presence; you have made me a homeless wanderer. Anyone who finds me will kill me!”
Anyone? If the story so far has only four people in it, and one of them is dead, who exactly is Cain afraid of? God doesn’t say there’s no one out there. Instead, he puts a mark on Cain to warn anyone who might try to kill him. And then the story continues:
“So Cain left the Lord’s presence and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden. Cain had sexual relations with his wife, and she became pregnant and gave birth to Enoch. Then Cain founded a city, which he named Enoch, after his son.”
A wife, appearing without introduction. A land with a name. And a city, which needs people to live in it.
Readers noticed this very early, and there are three main ways to explain it.
The first is the family answer, and it’s the oldest. A chapter later, Genesis says Adam had other sons and daughters. So Cain married his sister, and the people he feared were relatives, born over the long lifespans Genesis describes. An ancient Jewish book called Jubilees, written a couple of centuries before Jesus, even gives Cain’s wife a name: Awan. Islamic tradition tells a similar story. Early Muslim commentators reported that Eve gave birth to twins each time, a boy and a girl, and that each son was meant to marry the other’s twin sister. In that version, it’s a quarrel over which sister each brother would marry that leads the first son to kill the second.
The second explanation is the wider-population answer: that the story simply assumes there were other people around, beyond the family it names.
And the third comes from modern scholars who study how Genesis was put together. They suggest that the story of Cain may have preserved an older tradition about the origins of a particular people. The Kenites, a group that appears later in the Bible, may take their name from Cain, and the story may once have explained who they were and why they lived as wanderers. On that reading, the strangers and the city are part of a different story that was woven into this one. And it’s worth knowing that the Hebrew word translated “city” can mean something as small as a fortified settlement.
Now, none of these answers is proven by the text. Genesis leaves the details unexplained. But the questions were there from the start.
La Peyrère took the second answer and ran with it, and his doubts started somewhere unexpected: in a letter of Paul’s. In Romans chapter five, Paul writes that sin was in the world before the law was given, and that death reigned from Adam to Moses. La Peyrère read that as a hint that there had been people, and sin, before Adam. Most interpreters, then and now, read Paul differently. The law he means is the law of Moses, and his point is that death ruled even in the centuries before that law was given. La Peyrère’s reading was his own, and it’s one reason his critics found his argument so easy to dismiss.
He also noticed that Genesis tells the creation of humans twice. In chapter one, God creates humankind, male and female, in His image. In chapter two, He forms one man from the dust. Perhaps, La Peyrère argued, these were two separate creations. The first made the ancestors of the world’s nations. The second made Adam.
There’s a real feature of Hebrew behind that idea. The word adam can mean a human being, humankind as a whole, or the particular man named Adam, depending on the sentence. Genesis one could be talking about humanity in general, and Genesis two about one man.
But most biblical scholars today read the two chapters differently. They see two ancient accounts of the same beginning, each with its own style and emphasis, brought together when Genesis took its final form. Traditional readers have long seen chapter two as simply zooming in on the story chapter one told in outline. Either way, the two chapters don’t prove there were two creations of people. They show that the question has very old roots.
Five years before La Peyrère’s book, in 1650, the Irish archbishop James Ussher worked backward through the Bible’s genealogies and famously calculated that creation took place in 4004 BC. For a long time, that date was printed in the margins of English Bibles. But notice what it is. The Bible itself never gives a date for Adam. Ussher’s figure is a calculation, built on assumptions about how the genealogies should be read. It’s one interpretation, not a fact the text states.
And that matters, because there is one thing about the human past we can date with real confidence.
In 2017, researchers published new dates for fossils from a site in Morocco called Jebel Irhoud. They came from early members of our own species, Homo sapiens. Flint tools found with them had been heated in ancient fires, and heating resets a kind of natural clock inside the stone, which scientists can read. The answer was around three hundred thousand years. The faces looked strikingly like ours, though the backs of the skulls were longer and lower, a mix of the modern and the more ancient.
Our species wasn’t alone. Across Europe and western Asia lived the Neanderthals, a people adapted to the cold who made tools, controlled fire, and in some cases appear to have buried their dead. Further east lived a group we know mostly from their DNA, called the Denisovans. These weren’t failed drafts of humanity. They were other branches of the human family, with their own long histories. And when our ancestors spread out of Africa, they met these people, and had children with them. Most people alive today whose ancestry lies outside Africa carry a small trace of Neanderthal DNA, about one to two percent of their genome. Some populations, especially in Oceania, also carry Denisovan DNA.
And these early humans did far more than survive. On Muna, a small island off Sulawesi in Indonesia, someone pressed a hand against a cave wall and blew red pigment around it at least sixty-seven thousand years ago. Its discovery, announced in January 2026, makes it the oldest dated rock art in the world.
And in what is now southern Turkey, at a site called Göbekli Tepe, people raised enormous carved stone pillars in great circles around eleven and a half thousand years ago: before writing, before the wheel, and thousands of years before Ussher’s date for Adam. It was long described as the world’s first temple. Researchers now debate whether its buildings were purely places of ritual, or places where people also lived and worked. Either way, someone there was carving animals and symbols into stone with extraordinary care.
Then there’s the evidence inside our own cells. Every person carries a genome that’s slightly different from everyone else’s. By studying how much variety there is in human DNA today, and how it’s arranged, geneticists can work backward and estimate the size of the ancestral population it came from. That estimate, called the effective population, isn’t a headcount of everyone who lived. It’s closer to the number of breeding ancestors you’d need to produce the variety we see. And the variety in human DNA is too great, and too ancient, to have come from just two people in the last several hundred thousand years. Our ancestors were always a population. A few researchers have built mathematical models suggesting that a single founding couple much further back, hundreds of thousands of years ago, can’t be completely ruled out. That’s a possibility, not a finding, and we’ll come back to it.
Okay. So you may have heard of “Mitochondrial Eve” and “Y-chromosomal Adam.” They’re the most recent woman and man from whom everyone alive today inherits two particular strands of DNA, one passed down only through mothers, the other only through fathers. But they weren’t a couple, and they weren’t alone. Lots of people lived alongside each of them, and many of those people are our ancestors too. The names are a metaphor borrowed from Genesis. Science hasn’t found the biblical Adam and Eve.
We can date the fossils. We can’t date Adam. Science can tell us how ancient humanity is, but it can’t tell us where, or whether, Adam belongs on that timeline. So the question isn’t really whether there were humans before Adam. It’s which Adam we’re talking about.

Alright. Let’s start with the most traditional answer.
Many Christians, and many Muslims and Jews as well, hold that Adam and Eve were created directly by God, a few thousand years ago, and that every human being descends from them alone. For these believers, there were no humans before Adam. The question has a simple answer: no one.
They have serious reasons. The Bible’s genealogies run in an unbroken line from Adam to Abraham to David, tying him to real history. Jesus speaks of God creating humans male and female from the beginning, and mentions the blood of Abel as a real event. And Paul’s theology depends on one man. In Romans and in First Corinthians, he says that sin and death came through one man, Adam, and that life comes through one man, Christ. If Adam is only a symbol, these believers argue that the whole structure of the gospel loses its foundation. They also question the dating methods, arguing that they rest on assumptions about the distant past that can’t be directly tested.
Scientists, including many who are religious believers themselves, reply that the dates come from several independent methods that agree with each other, and that the genetic evidence doesn’t depend on fossils at all. And that’s why many thinkers in all three traditions have looked for another way. Some of those ways are much older than you might expect.
In Judaism, the idea that this world wasn’t the first goes back a very long way. An ancient collection of rabbinic commentary on Genesis, called Genesis Rabbah, records a teaching that God built worlds and destroyed them before He made this one. The Talmud even speaks of nine hundred and seventy-four generations that God had intended to create before the world, but did not. And later Jewish mystics developed the idea of earlier worlds into a whole theory of cosmic cycles, each world rising and ending before the next.
And in 1842, a respected rabbi named Israel Lipschitz gave a sermon in which he pointed to something new: fossils. Giant bones of extinct creatures were being dug up across Europe, and the frozen remains of a mammoth had been found in Siberia. To Lipschitz, these confirmed exactly what the ancient rabbis had taught. The world was older than Adam, and here were the remains of what came before. For him, science wasn’t a threat to the tradition. It was a confirmation.
Islam has its own version of this. In Surah 2, verse 30, God tells the angels He is going to place a successor on earth. The Arabic word is khalifah, a deputy or steward. And the angels reply with a question: will You place there one who will cause corruption and shed blood, while we praise You?
How did the angels know? Adam hadn’t done anything yet. Some classical Muslim commentators answered that the earth had already been inhabited, by the jinn, beings the Qur’an says in Surah 15, verse 27, were created from smokeless fire before humans, and that they had filled the earth with bloodshed. It’s important to be clear: the jinn aren’t a kind of prehistoric human. They’re a separate order of beings altogether. And other commentators explained the angels’ question differently, and see it as foresight given to them by God about the nature of the creature He was making.
A report in Shia tradition, attributed to the fifth Imam, Muhammad al-Baqir, goes further still. It says God created a thousand thousand worlds, and a thousand thousand Adams, and that we live in the last of those worlds, descended from the last of those Adams.
Still, the Qur’an’s main picture is clear. In Surah 4, verse 1, it says that God created humanity from a single soul and, from it, its mate. And in Surah 3, verse 59, it says that God created Adam from dust and said “Be,” and he was. Most Muslim scholars today hold that Adam was the first human being, created directly by God, and so the fossils raise real questions within Islamic thought too.
That brings us to the question at the heart of every attempt to reconcile the two. If there were other humans, what would make Adam different? His body? His language? His moral awareness? His relationship with God? Archaeology can tell us about tools, art and burials. It can’t tell us whether someone had a soul, or whether God ever spoke to them.
Some believers have proposed that Adam’s uniqueness lies exactly there.
In 1950, Pope Pius the Twelfth, in a document called Humani Generis, said Catholics were free to investigate whether the human body developed from earlier living forms, as long as they held that every human soul is created directly by God. He drew a firm line, though, at the idea that humanity descended from many first parents rather than one, because he didn’t see how that could fit with original sin.
Protestant writers have gone in several directions. The influential English pastor John Stott, building on a suggestion by the scholar Derek Kidner, proposed that Adam was a real man whom God chose from among existing humans, the first to receive God’s image and enter a relationship with Him. The philosopher William Lane Craig argued in 2021 for a very different answer: that Adam and Eve were real, but lived far earlier than anyone had assumed, perhaps more than seven hundred and fifty thousand years ago, as the founding pair of the whole human line, possibly members of an early human species known as Homo heidelbergensis. That’s a philosophical proposal, built around the kind of models we mentioned earlier. It shows such a couple is conceivable. It doesn’t show they existed.
And in Islam, the scholar Shoaib Ahmed Malik, drawing on the medieval theologian al-Ghazali, argued in 2021 that Muslims could accept that humans evolved, while holding that Adam and Eve were a miraculous exception: created directly by God, without parents, and placed in a world where other humans already lived, whose descendants later intermarried with theirs.
The most surprising idea of all came from a scientist. In 2019, a computational biologist named Joshua Swamidass pointed out a difference most of us never think about: the difference between your genetic ancestors and your genealogical ones.
Here’s how it works. You have two parents, four grandparents, eight great-grandparents, and the number doubles every generation. Go back far enough, and you have more ancestors than there were people alive. Those family lines overlap, so the same people turn up again and again in your tree. But you don’t inherit DNA from all of them. Each generation, some of their DNA drops out. So a person can be your ancestor without leaving a single trace in your genome.
Using computer models of how family trees spread, Swamidass argued that, under reasonable assumptions about how people moved and married, a couple who lived as recently as six thousand years ago in the Middle East could have become genealogical ancestors of every human alive today, even if many other people lived alongside them. They wouldn’t be the only source of our DNA. But they could be in everyone’s family tree.
That doesn’t prove Adam and Eve existed. It doesn’t say where or when they lived, or whether they were created miraculously. And theologians argue over whether it fits what Genesis, or the Qur’an, actually teaches. What it does show is that “ancestor of everyone” and “only person alive at the time” are not the same thing.
There’s one more way of approaching all of this, and it starts with a different question: what was the story of Adam originally trying to explain?
Many scholars point out that the Eden story is deeply concerned with the human condition. Why do we die? Why is work so hard? Why does giving birth hurt? Why do we feel shame? Why do we feel far from God? Stories that explain why things are the way they are were common in the ancient world, and on this reading, Genesis was answering those questions, not the questions a modern geneticist would ask. That doesn’t mean Adam must be fictional. Some believers hold that Adam was a real person, while accepting that his story is told in a symbolic way. Others see him as a representative of all humanity. And others read the account as straightforward history. But it does suggest we might be asking Genesis a question its first readers weren’t asking.
There’s a dark chapter in this story, though, and we have to tell it.
La Peyrère’s idea didn’t stay a theological curiosity. In the nineteenth century, it was picked up by people with a very different agenda. If some humans descended from Adam and others from people before him, then perhaps, they argued, not all humans belonged to the same family. In 1880, an American geologist named Alexander Winchell published a book arguing that Black people were a pre-Adamite race, older than Adam and, he claimed, inferior to Adam’s descendants. Before the American Civil War, theories that different races had separate origins were already being used to defend slavery. After it, books like Winchell’s lent a scientific-sounding authority to segregation, and to the claim that some peoples were less fully human than others.
And here’s the twist. The science that showed how ancient humanity is also demolished the racist use of that idea. Every human being alive today belongs to one species, sharing deep common ancestry. The genetic differences between populations are tiny, and most human variation exists within groups rather than between them. And genealogically, every one of us shares many of the same ancestors within the last few thousand years.
In Acts, Paul tells the philosophers of Athens:
“From one man he created all the nations throughout the whole earth.”
And the Qur’an says God created all people from a single soul. On the point that matters most for how we treat each other, the scriptures and the science stand in the same place. We are one human family.
Alright. So it’s time to weigh what we’ve found.
On the science, the case is closed beyond reasonable doubt. People of our own species lived at least three hundred thousand years ago. Other human lineages, like the Neanderthals and Denisovans, lived alongside them, and some of their DNA survives in us. People were making art, burying their dead and raising great monuments long before six thousand years ago. And our ancestors were always a population, not a single pair, for at least the last several hundred thousand years.
On the text, the issue is interpretation rather than evidence. Genesis mentions Cain’s fear of strangers, his wife and his city without explaining where they came from. The traditional answer points to Adam’s other children. Others see a wider world, or an older story woven in. And the Bible never gives Adam a date. Ussher’s six thousand years is a calculation, not a statement of scripture.
Genuinely contested, in both the lab and the seminary: whether a single founding couple far back in time can be ruled out, and whether Adam was the first biological human, a man chosen from among others, a founding father in the deep past, or a figure who represents us all.
And what is out of any scientist’s reach is whether the earliest humans had souls, and what it means to bear the image of God.
Putting all of that together, this is my verdict.
Who were the ancient humans? On the evidence, they were us. People of our own species, and close cousins like the Neanderthals, who made tools, painted, mourned their dead, and very likely looked up at the sky and wondered. They weren’t lesser beings. They were part of the same story we belong to.
Were they before Adam? That depends on which Adam you mean, and that’s the real discovery here. If Adam lived around six thousand years ago, as Ussher calculated, then there were humans long before him, and that is about as certain as anything in ancient history. What the evidence makes very hard to hold is the idea that, within the last several hundred thousand years, every human descended from just two people and no one else. Our own DNA doesn’t fit that picture.
But that doesn’t close the case on Adam. A real Adam chosen from among existing humans, a founding pair in the deep past, or a man whose family tree eventually reached everyone alive: none of these can be ruled out by the fossils or the genes. And none of them can be proven by them either. We can date the bones. We can’t date Adam. Whether he was the beginning of human life, or the beginning of humanity’s life with God, is the point where evidence stops and faith has to decide.
