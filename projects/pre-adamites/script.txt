Who Were the Humans Before Adam and Eve?
Hey there! Here’s a question almost every kid asks in Sunday school. Adam and Eve have two sons, Cain and Abel. Cain kills Abel. So now there are three people on earth. Then Cain gets scared. He tells God, “Anyone who finds me will kill me!” Wait. Anyone? Who? Then Cain gets married. To who? Then he builds a city. For who?
In 1655, a French scholar named Isaac La Peyrère asked exactly that. And his answer was that there must have been people before Adam. His book got burned. He got arrested. And he ended up in Rome, taking it all back in front of Pope Alexander the Seventh.
But here’s the twist. Today, science says there really were people on earth long before the date tradition gave for Adam. Hundreds of thousands of years before.
So who were they? Does that mean Adam and Eve weren’t the first humans? And what do Christians, Muslims and Jews actually say about it? In this video, we’ll look at what the Bible and the Qur’an really say about the first people, what the bones and the DNA show, and how all three faiths try to make it all fit. Let’s get into it.
Okay, first, why does this even matter? Because Adam isn’t just a name at the start of a family tree. For Christians, Adam is where sin entered the world. The apostle Paul builds his whole explanation of salvation on that. For Muslims, Adam is the first human and the first prophet. He’s the one God taught the names of all things. And for Jews, he’s where the human story in the Torah begins. So if there were people before Adam, or living alongside him, all three faiths have to ask a big question. What exactly was Adam?
Let’s start with the story itself. You probably know it. In Genesis, God forms a man from the dust of the ground and breathes life into him. He puts him in a garden. Then He makes a woman from the man’s side. The man later names her Eve, and Genesis says she would be the mother of all who live. They eat the forbidden fruit. They get kicked out of Eden. They have two sons. And then Cain kills his brother.
God punishes Cain by sending him away as a wanderer. And that’s when Cain says this:
“You have banished me from the land and from your presence; you have made me a homeless wanderer. Anyone who finds me will kill me!”
Notice what God does next. He doesn’t say, “Relax, Cain, there’s nobody out there.” Instead, He puts a mark on Cain, to warn anyone who might try to kill him. And then the story keeps going:
“So Cain left the Lord’s presence and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden. Cain had sexual relations with his wife, and she became pregnant and gave birth to Enoch. Then Cain founded a city, which he named Enoch, after his son.”
So, in a few sentences, we get a wife nobody introduced, a land that already has a name, and a city. And cities need people.
Now, you might be thinking: surely somebody noticed this before? Oh, absolutely. People have been asking about Cain’s wife for over two thousand years. And there are three main answers.
The first one is the family answer, and it’s the oldest. A chapter later, Genesis says Adam had other sons and daughters. So Cain married one of his sisters, and the people he was afraid of were his own relatives. Yes, that sounds strange to us today. But in the story, the first family had nobody else to marry, and no law against it yet. An ancient Jewish book called Jubilees, written a couple of centuries before Jesus, even gives Cain’s wife a name. Awan.
Islamic tradition tells a very similar story. Early Muslim commentators said Eve gave birth to twins every time, a boy and a girl. Each son was supposed to marry the other son’s twin sister. And in their version, it’s actually a fight over which sister each brother would marry that leads to the very first murder.
The second answer is simpler. Maybe the story just assumes there were other people around, beyond the family it tells us about.
And the third answer comes from scholars who study how Genesis was put together. They think the story of Cain might have started out as an older story about where a particular group of people came from. Later in the Bible, there’s a group called the Kenites, and their name may come from Cain. So the strangers and the city might be pieces of that older story, stitched into this one. And here’s a fun detail. The Hebrew word we translate as “city” can mean something much smaller. Think less New York, more a walled-in village.
So which one is right? Honestly, Genesis doesn’t say. It leaves the details open. But the questions were there from the very start.
La Peyrère went with the second answer. And the first thing that made him suspicious wasn’t even in Genesis. It was in a letter from Paul. In Romans chapter five, Paul says sin was in the world before the law was given, and that death ruled from Adam to Moses. La Peyrère read that and thought, wait, does that mean people were sinning before Adam? Most readers, then and now, don’t see it that way. They say Paul means the law of Moses, and his point is that death ruled even in the centuries before that law arrived. So La Peyrère was pretty much on his own there. That’s one reason his critics found his book so easy to shoot down.
But he noticed something else that’s more interesting. Genesis tells the creation of humans twice. In chapter one, God creates humankind, male and female, all at once. Then in chapter two, He forms one man from the dust. So La Peyrère asked, what if those were two different creations? First, God made lots of people all over the world. Then, later, He made Adam.
And there’s actually something in the Hebrew behind that idea. The word adam can mean a human being. It can mean all of humanity. Or it can mean the particular man named Adam. It depends on the sentence. So you could read chapter one as being about humanity in general, and chapter two as being about one man.
But most scholars today read it differently. They think the two chapters are two old accounts of the same beginning, each written in its own style, and later put side by side. Traditional readers usually see chapter two as zooming in on what chapter one gave as a quick overview. Kind of like a movie that shows you the whole city from above, and then cuts to one house. So the two chapters don’t prove there were two creations. They just show this question has very deep roots.
Okay, so now, when was Adam supposed to have lived? In 1650, an Irish archbishop named James Ussher sat down with the Bible’s family trees. He added up all the ages and generations, and he worked out that creation happened in 4004 BC. That’s about six thousand years ago. For a long time, that date was even printed in the margins of English Bibles.
But here’s the thing. The Bible never actually gives a date for Adam. Ussher’s number is a calculation. It depends on how you read those family trees. So it’s one interpretation, not something the text says.
And that matters a lot, because there’s one thing about the human past we can date pretty confidently. The bones.
In 2017, scientists published new dates for fossils found at a place in Morocco called Jebel Irhoud. They belonged to early members of our own species, Homo sapiens. And they were about three hundred thousand years old.
Now, you might be wondering, how on earth do you date something that old? Here’s a simple way to picture it. Some stones slowly soak up tiny bits of natural energy from the ground around them, kind of like a battery charging, very, very slowly. When a stone gets heated in a fire, that battery gets reset to zero. Then it starts charging again. So when scientists find flint tools that were dropped into an ancient campfire, they can measure how much energy has built up since, and work out how long ago that fire burned. That’s how they dated these fossils.
And the faces? They looked a lot like ours. The backs of the skulls were longer and lower, a bit more old-fashioned. But if one of these people sat next to you on the bus with a haircut and a hoodie, you’d probably just think they had a strong jaw.
And our species wasn’t alone. Across Europe and western Asia lived the Neanderthals. They were tough, cold-adapted people who made tools, used fire, and in some cases seem to have buried their dead. Further east lived a group we mostly know from their DNA, called the Denisovans. And no, these weren’t failed attempts at being human. They were other branches of the human family, with their own long histories.
Here’s where it gets wild. When our ancestors moved out of Africa, they met these people. And they had children together. So most people alive today whose families come from outside Africa still carry a little Neanderthal DNA. About two percent. Some people, especially in places like Papua New Guinea, also carry Denisovan DNA. So, in a way, a few of those ancient cousins are still with us.
And these early humans weren’t just surviving. They were creating. On a small island in Indonesia called Muna, someone once pressed their hand against a cave wall and sprayed red pigment around it. That handprint is at least sixty-seven thousand years old. When it was announced in January 2026, it became the oldest dated rock art in the world. The runner-up? A hand stencil in a cave in Spain, which researchers think was made by Neanderthals.
And then there’s Göbekli Tepe, in what’s now southern Turkey. Around eleven and a half thousand years ago, people there raised huge carved stone pillars in great circles. That’s before writing. Before the wheel. And thousands of years before Ussher’s date for Adam. It used to be called the world’s first temple. Today, researchers argue about whether it was mainly a place of worship, or a place where people also lived and worked. Either way, someone was carving foxes, snakes and vultures into stone with a lot of care.
Now, for the evidence, it’s hiding inside you. Literally. Your DNA.
Everyone’s DNA is a little bit different. Scientists can look at how much variety there is in human DNA today, and use it to work backward. Here’s a simple way to think about it. Imagine a giant bag of marbles in hundreds of different colours. If that bag had been filled from just two small jars, you’d only see a few colours. But if you see hundreds of colours, all mixed in complicated patterns, you know the bag was filled from a lot of different jars, over a long time.
Human DNA looks like the second bag. There’s too much variety, and it’s too old to have come from just two people in the last several hundred thousand years. Our ancestors were always a group, a population, not one couple.
Now, a few researchers have built mathematical models suggesting that one founding couple much, much further back, hundreds of thousands of years ago, can’t be completely ruled out. But that’s a possibility, not a discovery. We’ll come back to it.
And wait, you might ask, what about “Mitochondrial Eve” and “Y-chromosomal Adam”? Didn’t scientists find them? Not really. Here’s the trick. Think of surnames. In a lot of families, the surname gets passed from father to son. If a man only has daughters, his surname line ends, even though he still has descendants. Over hundreds of generations, lots of surnames disappear, until only a few are left. That doesn’t mean only a few families ever existed.
Y-chromosomal Adam is like the one man whose “surname” in the DNA happened to survive along the father-to-son line. Mitochondrial Eve is the same idea, through mothers to daughters. They weren’t a couple. They lived tens of thousands of years apart. And plenty of other people lived alongside each of them, who are also our ancestors. Scientists just borrowed the names from Genesis because they’re catchy.
So, here’s where it gets interesting. We can date the fossils. We can’t date Adam. Science can tell us how old humanity is. But it can’t tell us where Adam fits on that timeline, or even whether he’s on it. So the real question isn’t “were there humans before Adam?” It’s “which Adam are we talking about?”
Alright. Let’s start with the most traditional answer.
Many Christians, and lots of Muslims and Jews too, believe Adam and Eve were created directly by God a few thousand years ago, and that every single human comes from them alone. For them, the answer to “who were the humans before Adam?” is simple. Nobody.
And they have good reasons for believing it. The Bible’s family trees run in one unbroken line from Adam, to Abraham, to David. That makes Adam feel like real history. Jesus talks about God creating people male and female from the beginning, and he mentions Abel’s death as a real event. And Paul’s whole argument rests on one man. In Romans and in First Corinthians, he says sin and death came through one man, Adam, and life comes through one man, Christ. So these believers ask, if Adam was just a symbol, doesn’t the whole thing fall apart? They also question the dating methods, saying they depend on assumptions about the distant past that nobody can check directly.
Scientists, including many who are believers themselves, reply that several completely different dating methods give the same answers. And the DNA evidence doesn’t depend on fossils at all. That’s why lots of thinkers in all three faiths have looked for another way. And some of those ways are much older than you’d expect.
Take Judaism. The idea that this world wasn’t the first one goes back a long way. An ancient collection of rabbinic teaching on Genesis, called Genesis Rabbah, says God built worlds and destroyed them before He made this one. The Talmud even talks about nine hundred and seventy-four generations that God planned to create before the world, but didn’t. And later Jewish mystics turned the idea of earlier worlds into a whole theory of cosmic cycles. One world rises and ends, then the next one begins.
And here’s my favourite part. In 1842, a respected rabbi named Israel Lipschitz gave a sermon about fossils. People across Europe were digging up giant bones of creatures nobody had ever seen, and a frozen mammoth had turned up in Siberia. Lots of religious people were nervous about these discoveries. Not Lipschitz. He basically said, “See? The rabbis told us. The world is older than Adam, and these are the leftovers of what came before.” For him, science wasn’t a threat. It was a confirmation.
Islam has its own version of this puzzle. In the second chapter of the Qur’an, God tells the angels He’s going to place a successor on earth. The Arabic word is khalifah. Think of it as a steward, or a deputy. And the angels ask a surprising question. Will You place there someone who will cause corruption and shed blood, while we praise You?
Wait. How did the angels know about bloodshed? Adam hadn’t even been created yet. Some early Muslim commentators said the earth already had inhabitants before Adam. Not humans, but jinn. These are beings the Qur’an says were made from smokeless fire, before humans, and some commentators said the jinn had filled the earth with violence. To be clear, the jinn aren’t cavemen. They’re a completely separate kind of being. Other commentators explained it differently. They said God simply let the angels see what kind of creature humans would be.
And there’s an even bigger idea. A report in Shia tradition, linked to the fifth Imam, Muhammad al-Baqir, says God created a thousand thousand worlds, and a thousand thousand Adams. And we live in the last of those worlds, descended from the last of those Adams.
Still, the Qur’an’s main picture is clear. It says God created all people from a single soul, and from it, its mate. And it says God created Adam from dust and said “Be,” and he was. Most Muslim scholars today believe Adam was the first human, made directly by God. So the fossils raise real questions in Islamic thought too.
Which brings us to the big question underneath all of this. If there were other humans around, what would make Adam special? His body? His language? His sense of right and wrong? His relationship with God? Here’s the catch. Archaeology can find tools, paintings and graves. But it can’t dig up a soul. And it can’t tell us whether God ever spoke to someone.
And that’s exactly where some believers say Adam’s uniqueness lies.
In 1950, Pope Pius the Twelfth wrote a document called Humani Generis. He said Catholics were free to explore whether the human body developed from earlier living things, as long as they believed every human soul is created directly by God. But he drew a line at the idea that humans came from lots of first parents instead of one, because he couldn’t see how that fit with original sin.
Protestant thinkers have gone in a few directions. A famous English pastor named John Stott, building on an idea from the scholar Derek Kidner, suggested that Adam was a real man God chose from among humans who already existed. The first to bear God’s image, and the first to know Him. Then, in 2021, the philosopher William Lane Craig suggested something very different. He argued that Adam and Eve were real, but lived way, way earlier than anyone thought. Maybe more than seven hundred and fifty thousand years ago, as the founding pair of the whole human line. Possibly part of an early human species called Homo heidelbergensis. That’s a philosophical proposal, built around those mathematical models we mentioned earlier. It shows a couple like that is possible to imagine. It doesn’t show they existed.
And in Islam, a scholar named Shoaib Ahmed Malik, drawing on the medieval theologian al-Ghazali, argued in 2021 that Muslims could accept that humans evolved, while still believing Adam and Eve were a miracle. Made directly by God, without parents, and placed into a world where other humans already lived. Over time, their families and the other families would have mixed.
Now, the most surprising idea of all actually came from a scientist. In 2019, a computational biologist named Joshua Swamidass pointed out the difference between two kinds of ancestors. Your genetic ancestors, and your family-tree ancestors.
Here’s how it works. You have two parents. Four grandparents. Eight great-grandparents. Every generation back, the number doubles. Go back far enough, and you’d have more ancestors than there were people alive. That’s impossible, right? Unless the same people show up in your family tree again and again. And they do. Family trees overlap like crazy. But here’s the twist. You don’t get DNA from all of them. Some of it drops out every generation, like a game of telephone where some words just get lost. So someone can be your ancestor without leaving a single trace in your DNA.
Using computer models, Swamidass argued that, if you make reasonable guesses about how people moved and married, a couple living around six thousand years ago in the Middle East could have ended up in the family tree of every person alive today. Even if lots of other people lived alongside them. They wouldn’t be the source of all our DNA. But they could be everyone’s ancestors.
Now, does that prove Adam and Eve existed? No. It doesn’t say where or when they lived. It doesn’t say God made them by a miracle. And theologians still argue about whether it fits what Genesis or the Qur’an actually teach. What it does show is this. Being “everyone’s ancestor” isn’t the same as being “the only person alive at the time.”
There’s one more way to look at all this. And it starts with a different question. What was the story of Adam trying to explain in the first place?
A lot of scholars point out that the Eden story is really focused on the big human questions. Why do we die? Why is work so hard? Why does giving birth hurt? Why do we feel shame? Why do we feel far from God? Stories that explain “why things are the way they are” were really common in the ancient world. So maybe Genesis was answering those questions, not the questions a modern scientist would ask.
That doesn’t mean Adam has to be made up. Some believers think Adam was a real man, and his story is told in a symbolic way. Others see him as a stand-in for all of humanity. And others read it as straightforward history. But it does suggest we might be asking Genesis a question its first readers weren’t asking.
Okay. Now there’s a dark side to this story. And we have to talk about it.
La Peyrère’s idea didn’t stay a quiet theological debate. In the 1800s, some people picked it up for an ugly purpose. They argued that if some humans came from Adam and others came from people before him, then maybe not everyone belonged to the same human family. In 1880, an American geologist named Alexander Winchell wrote a book claiming that Black people came from races that existed before Adam, and stood outside his family line. Even before the American Civil War, theories that different races had separate origins were being used to defend slavery. After it, books like Winchell’s gave a scientific-sounding cover to segregation, and to the claim that some people were less human than others.
But here’s the twist. The same science that showed humanity is ancient also destroyed that racist idea. Every human alive today belongs to one species. We all share deep common roots. The genetic differences between groups of people are tiny. Most of the variety actually exists within groups, not between them. And if you trace family trees back just a few thousand years, all of us share many of the same ancestors.
In the Book of Acts, Paul tells the thinkers of Athens:
“From one man he created all the nations throughout the whole earth.”
And the Qur’an says God created all people from a single soul. So on the question that matters most for how we treat each other, the scriptures and the science end up standing in the same place. We’re one human family.
So, in conclusion. Where does all of this leave us?
Some things are about as certain as ancient history gets. People of our own species were around at least three hundred thousand years ago. Other kinds of humans, like Neanderthals and Denisovans, lived alongside them, and a bit of their DNA is still in us. People were making art, burying their dead and building huge stone monuments long before six thousand years ago. And our ancestors were always a group, not a single pair, for at least the last several hundred thousand years.
Some things come down to how you read the text. Genesis mentions Cain’s fear, his wife and his city, without explaining where those people came from. The traditional answer says Adam’s other children. Others see a wider world, or an older story woven in. And the Bible never gives Adam a date. Ussher’s six thousand years is a calculation, not a verse.
Some things are still being argued about. Could there have been one founding couple way back in deep time? And was Adam the first human body, a man God chose from among others, a founding father long ago, or a figure who stands for all of us?
And some things no scientist can ever reach. Did those earliest people have souls? And what does it mean to be made in the image of God?
So here’s where I land.
Who were the ancient humans? They were us. People of our own species, and close cousins like the Neanderthals. They made tools. They painted. They mourned their dead. And they very likely looked up at the night sky and wondered. They weren’t lesser beings. They were part of the same story we’re in.
Were they before Adam? That depends on which Adam you mean. And honestly, that’s the biggest discovery in this whole investigation. If Adam lived six thousand years ago, like Ussher calculated, then yes. There were humans long before him, and that’s about as certain as anything in ancient history gets. What our DNA makes really hard to believe is that everyone in the last several hundred thousand years came from just two people and nobody else.
But that doesn’t close the case on Adam. A real Adam chosen from among other humans. A founding couple in the deep past. A man whose family tree eventually reached everyone alive. Fossils and genes can’t rule any of those out. And they can’t prove them either. We can date the bones. We can’t date Adam. Whether he was the start of human life, or the start of humanity’s life with God, is where the evidence stops, and faith has to decide.
