Hey there! Let’s say you ordered something from the other side of the world. A new phone, a pair of sneakers, maybe a sofa. The tracking page says “In transit.” A week later, it still says “In transit.” Two weeks later? Same thing.

What the tracking page doesn’t tell you is that the ship carrying your order might be in serious trouble.

On June 17, 2013, a container ship called the MOL Comfort was sailing from Singapore to Jeddah, in Saudi Arabia, when its hull cracked in bad weather in the Indian Ocean. Then the ship broke completely in two. All 26 crew members were rescued. But the two halves of the ship were still floating. And on board were 4,382 containers, carrying cargo for businesses around the world.

So, what happens to all that cargo if those two halves sink? Does the shipping company pay everyone back? Can someone go down and get the containers? And if somebody finds that cargo years later, who actually owns it? In this video, we’ll follow the cargo after a container ship goes down, from the bottom of the ocean all the way to the offices of some of the world’s biggest insurance companies.

First, let’s clear something up. When a ship sinks, not every container goes down with it, at least not right away.

Normally, containers are locked in place. On deck, they’re stacked and held together with steel twist locks and lashing rods. Below deck, they slide into steel frames called cell guides, a bit like books on a tight bookshelf. But when a ship breaks apart, all of that can fail.

So, a few different things can happen. Some containers stay trapped inside the ship and go down with it. Some break free and sink on their own, sometimes far from the wreck. And some float. A container might look like a solid steel box, but it’s really a box full of air and cargo. It’s built to keep out rain and sea spray, not to stay sealed underwater. Depending on what’s inside, it can float for days, weeks, or even months, often riding just below the surface, where smaller boats can’t see it until it’s too late.

Now, before you cancel every online order you’ve ever made, let’s put this in perspective. According to the World Shipping Council, about 1,478 containers were lost at sea in 2025. That sounds like a lot. But it’s out of roughly 280 million containers moved that year. That’s about one container lost for every 189,000 shipped. And not all of those containers were lost because ships sank. Containers can also be knocked overboard in storms while the ship carrying them keeps sailing.

But when containers do end up in the water, some of them break open. And that’s when the ocean starts delivering packages to places nobody ordered them.

In 1990, a storm knocked containers off a ship in the North Pacific, and tens of thousands of Nike shoes spilled out. When they started washing up on beaches in the American Northwest, an oceanographer in Seattle named Curtis Ebbesmeyer realized he could use them to study ocean currents. Then, in 1992, another container broke open in the North Pacific and released 28,800 plastic bath toys. Yellow ducks, red beavers, blue turtles and green frogs. About ten months later, the first ones washed up in Alaska, roughly 2,000 miles away. So yeah, some serious ocean science has been powered by rubber ducks.

And in 1997, a huge wave hit a ship called the Tokio Express off the coast of Cornwall, in England, knocking 62 containers overboard. One of them held about 4.8 million pieces of Lego. And here’s the funny part: a lot of them were sea-themed. Tiny scuba tanks, flippers, octopuses. Almost three decades later, people are still finding them on Cornish beaches.

But even if a container stays in one piece, that doesn’t mean the goods inside have survived.

Let’s imagine four containers on the same sinking ship. The first is full of electronics. Salt water gets into the circuits, and they corrode. Even if they dry out, nobody wants to sell a phone that’s been at the bottom of the sea. The second is a refrigerated container full of food. The moment it loses power, it loses temperature control, and the food can spoil or become unsafe. The third holds industrial machinery. Some of it might be saved, but cleaning, inspecting and repairing it could cost more than it’s worth. And the fourth is full of tiny plastic pellets, the raw material used to make plastic products. If those bags burst, the pellets can spread across huge stretches of ocean and coastline.

And then there’s depth. The back half of the MOL Comfort eventually went down in water about 4,000 meters deep. At that depth, the water pressure is roughly 400 times what you feel at sea level.

So, what does a container look like down there? Well, in 2004, a ship called the Med Taipei lost 24 containers in a storm off California. A few months later, researchers from the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute found one of them by accident, lying upside down on the seabed, about 1,281 meters deep. It was full of more than a thousand car tires. When scientists came back years later, deep-sea animals had settled on it. But the sea life on the seafloor around it had changed, across an area more than 20 times the size of the container itself.

So here’s the first big lesson. A shipment can become worthless even when it still exists.

Now, you might be thinking, “Okay, Joe, but if those containers are full of valuable stuff, why not just send another ship to go and get them?”

Good question. And sometimes, that does happen. But first, someone has to find them. That might mean searching huge areas with sonar, then sending down robotic submarines called ROVs. Then you need specialist ships, cranes, divers or robots, calm enough weather, and a wreck that won’t shift or collapse while you’re working on it. Every single day of that costs money.

So let’s do the math with an example. Let’s say a container held $100,000 worth of goods. After months in salt water, what’s left might be worth $15,000. But getting that container up from the seabed could cost, say, $80,000. So, if you bring it up, you spend $80,000 to recover $15,000. You’ve just lost another $65,000 on top of the original loss. From a business point of view, the cargo often stays where it is.

Now, when a wreck is easier to reach, recovery really does happen. In October 2011, a container ship called the Rena ran aground on a reef about 12 nautical miles off New Zealand, carrying 1,368 containers. Salvage teams spent months lifting containers off the ship, even after it broke in two in January 2012, and more than a thousand were eventually recovered. But the Rena was stuck on a reef near the coast. The back half of the MOL Comfort was four kilometers down.

That’s also why professional salvage is such a gamble. A lot of it is done under a contract called Lloyd’s Open Form, which works on a principle called “no cure, no pay.” If the salvors fail, they usually get nothing. If they succeed, they’re rewarded based partly on the value of what they saved. There are special arrangements for protecting the environment, but the basic idea is simple: no rescue, no reward.

Okay. So if the cargo is gone, and nobody’s bringing it back, who actually pays for it?

If you’re just waiting for an online order, the store you bought from will usually have to sort things out with you. But imagine you’re the business that imported the goods in the first place. That’s where it gets complicated.

Let’s say you run a small business, and you’ve imported $100,000 worth of furniture from Asia. Your container is on a ship that sinks. So, who owes you $100,000?

Your first thought is probably, “The shipping company, obviously. They lost my container.”

Well, not quite. When your goods go on a ship, the shipping company gives you a document called a bill of lading. It’s basically a receipt and a contract in one. And that contract usually comes with international rules that limit what the shipping company has to pay.

First, a carrier may have a legal defense for certain losses caused by what are called “perils of the sea,” like exceptionally severe weather. But a storm doesn’t automatically excuse a carrier that was careless. And even when the carrier is to blame, its liability is usually capped. In the United States, for example, a law called the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act generally limits the carrier’s liability to $500 per package, unless a higher value was declared and agreed. Now, what counts as a “package” can get complicated, so it isn’t automatically $500 for your whole container. But either way, it can come nowhere near what your furniture was actually worth.

So, what actually protects you? Insurance. And in shipping, there are three main kinds.

The ship itself is covered by hull and machinery insurance. The shipowner’s responsibilities to other people, things like pollution, injuries, removing a wreck, and some cargo claims, are usually covered by something called P&I insurance, short for protection and indemnity. And your goods are covered by marine cargo insurance, if you bought it.

That last one is the one that matters to you. If you have it, you report the loss, send in your invoices and shipping documents, and the insurer checks the claim against your policy. If it’s covered, they pay you, minus any deductible. Then, if they think the shipping company was at fault, the insurer can go after it themselves. That’s called subrogation. Basically, they pay you first, then chase the money on their own.

But even good insurance doesn’t cover everything. It usually pays for the goods themselves, not the sales you missed, the customers you disappointed, or the extra cost of rushing in replacements. And if you didn’t buy cargo insurance at all? Then you might just be out $100,000. Yeah, painful.

But here’s where it gets really strange. Let’s change the story. This time, the ship doesn’t sink. It catches fire. Salvage teams fight the fire for days and save the ship. Your container was far from the flames, and your furniture is completely fine.

Good news, right? Well, you might still get a bill.

This is called general average, and it’s one of the oldest rules in the world. Imagine an ancient merchant ship in a storm. To keep it afloat, the captain throws some cargo overboard. It works. But one merchant just lost his goods to save everyone else’s. So, everyone whose cargo survived chips in to cover his loss. That idea goes back thousands of years, to the sea laws of the island of Rhodes, and it’s still used today under a set of rules called the York-Antwerp Rules.

Here’s how it works now. If something is deliberately sacrificed, or extraordinary costs are paid, to save the ship and cargo from a shared danger, everyone who benefited shares the bill, in proportion to the value of what they had on board. Now, it’s important to be clear: general average isn’t automatic every time a ship sinks. If the ship and cargo are simply lost, there’s usually nothing saved to share the cost. It applies when something was saved.

And the amounts can be huge. In March 2018, the container ship Maersk Honam caught fire in the Arabian Sea. Five crew members lost their lives. Salvage teams saved the ship and part of the cargo, and general average was declared. Before shippers could get their surviving cargo back, they were asked for security worth 42.5% of the cargo’s CIF value, that’s the cost of the goods plus insurance and freight, for the salvors, plus another 11.5% for general average. That’s 54% in total. Those were security requirements, not the final bill. But imagine your $100,000 container came through without a scratch, and you were asked for $54,000 in security just to get it released. If your cargo insurance covers it, your insurer can usually provide that guarantee instead. If not, it’s on you.

And this isn’t just old history. General average was declared after the Ever Given got stuck in the Suez Canal in 2021, and again after the Dali hit Baltimore’s Key Bridge in 2024.

Now, let’s go back to the cargo that does make it out of the sea. Say a container washes up on a beach. Can you keep what’s inside?

In January 2007, a container ship called the MSC Napoli was badly damaged in a storm and deliberately run aground off the coast of Devon, in England. Containers washed up on a beach near a small village called Branscombe. Inside were wine barrels, car parts, perfume, diapers, and brand-new BMW motorbikes. Hundreds of people showed up and started carrying things away. One group even used a tractor.

But under British law, anything that washes ashore from a wreck still belongs to its owner. Finders have to report it to an official called the Receiver of Wreck within 28 days, and police warned that keeping things without reporting them could be treated like theft. In the end, many of the people who did report their finds were told they could keep them. Even some of the motorbikes. But only because they followed the rules.

And the rules vary from country to country. In general, though, finding cargo doesn’t make it yours. It might still belong to the original owner, or to an insurer that already paid out for it. A finder might earn a salvage reward, but that’s not the same as owning it. And here’s a twist: the steel box and the goods inside might belong to completely different companies.

So far, we’ve talked about cargo losing value. But sometimes, it’s the opposite. Sometimes the cargo becomes far more expensive than the ship.

In May 2021, a container ship called the X-Press Pearl caught fire off the coast of Sri Lanka, and sank after burning for about two weeks. On board were chemicals, including about 25 tons of nitric acid, and an estimated 70 billion tiny plastic pellets. They washed up along Sri Lanka’s coastline in what’s been described as the world’s worst marine plastic spill. Fishing communities were hit hard. And in July 2025, Sri Lanka’s Supreme Court ordered the ship’s owners, operators and local agent to pay an initial $1 billion in compensation.

That’s also why some wrecks have to be dealt with, whether there’s money in it or not. Since 2015, an international treaty called the Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention has made registered shipowners financially responsible for locating, marking and removing hazardous wrecks, wherever the convention applies. It also requires qualifying ships with a gross tonnage of 300 or more to carry insurance or other financial security to cover that liability. So sometimes cargo is removed, not because it’s worth anything, but because leaving it there would be dangerous.

So, what finally happened to the MOL Comfort?

On June 27, 2013, the back half sank in water about 4,000 meters deep, taking around 1,700 containers with it. Some were seen floating nearby. Tugboats were still towing the front half when, on July 6, it caught fire. On July 10, it sank too, in about 3,000 meters of water, with around 2,400 more containers.

And with that, the hope of saving the cargo was over. For businesses around the world waiting for their shipments, the story moved from the ocean to the paperwork. Claims, invoices, policies and lawyers. The International Union of Marine Insurance estimated the insured cargo loss at between $300 million and $400 million.

So, what’s the big lesson here? When a container ship sinks, the cargo may stop moving. But the money, the paperwork and the responsibility keep travelling around the world. Some cargo floats away and turns up on beaches decades later. Some becomes a strange new home on the seabed. And almost all of it ends with someone paying a bill. Because global shipping isn’t just a system built to move goods. It’s also a system built to deal with what happens when those goods never arrive.
